Optical Quantum Computing

Independent research TJHSST 2025 – Present

Designing and building low-cost optical tweezer arrays for neutral-atom qubits — and the quantum computer that will use them.

The idea

Neutral-atom quantum computers hold individual atoms in tightly focused beams of laser light — optical tweezers — and use those atoms as qubits. It's one of the most promising routes to scalable quantum hardware, and also one of the most demanding: the optics, vacuum, and control all have to come together with extraordinary precision.

That difficulty is exactly what drew me in. Building the stack myself, at a high-school budget, gives me a ground-level appreciation for the engineering challenges that make neutral-atom computing so hard — and so promising.

What I've built

  • Independently designed atomic traps for rubidium and cesium.
  • Built functional optical tweezers that are now entering their experimental testing phase.
  • Designed my own version of an optical quantum computer — tuned to stay financially practical for high schools and small universities, yet large enough to process real projects and code.

Foundations

I studied Electrodynamics & Quantum Physics under Mr. Mark Hannum, alongside a university-level math and physics load — AP Physics C (Mechanics and E&M), Multivariable Calculus, and Advanced Mathematical Techniques.

To pressure-test my conceptual understanding, I sat the Quantum Mechanics Conceptual Survey (QMCS) — a benchmark normally given to upper-level undergraduates and introductory PhD students — and scored 40% against a typical mean of about 35%.

This fall

I've received approval to begin constructing a quantum computer in the TJHSST labs this coming fall — the next step in turning these benchtop pieces into a working machine.

Tools

Quantum simulation in PennyLane, Qiskit, and Nighthawk; CAD in Fusion 360 and Inventor; and tight-tolerance parts produced on CNC machines and both high- and low-end 3-D printers.

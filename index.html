The main thing

I'm building a quantum computer. In my high school.

Neutral-atom machines trap single atoms in laser light and use them as qubits. I'm building that whole stack myself — on a budget a school could actually afford.

I design optical tweezers — tightly focused laser beams that hold one rubidium or cesium atom at a time — and I've got a working set entering its testing phase. The plan is a small optical quantum computer cheap enough for a high school or small university, but big enough to run actual code.

This fall, I'm cleared to start building one for real in the TJ labs. (I also once scored 40% on a quantum-mechanics survey written for PhD students. Sounds rough — until you hear the average is 35%.)

About

The short version

I'm a junior at Thomas Jefferson High School for Science and Technology, and I spend an unreasonable amount of my free time aligning optics for atom traps I build myself.

Most of what I do starts the same way: a hard problem, a pile of parts, and the stubborn belief that a high-schooler can build the real thing — not a model of it. That's how I ended up with neutral-atom tweezers, a liquid-fuel rocket, an accessibility startup, two finance papers, and a provisional patent.

When I'm away from a lab or a CAD file, I'm playing viola, 3-D printing something, cooking, or arguing a point in Model UN.
